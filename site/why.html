Back in 2023 I joined the Kairos team. At the time I had never heard of immutable operating systems, so I was eager to learn. Beyond the documentation of each project, I could not find many sources. I wanted a bird's-eye view, one that would show me the patterns and not only how each project does things on its own. The first post I read was my colleague Nicolas Vermande's k3OS Alternatives: Top Container OS for Edge Kubernetes on the Spectro Cloud blog. It is a great read if you already know the projects it mentions, but it assumes you do. And if you are reading it today, it has aged.

Another good source I found was the CNCF Special Purpose Operating System Working Group, under what was then TAG Runtime. I joined, and I had a great time learning about the different projects. We even put together a couple of panels: one at KubeCon, and one at Open Source Summit Europe in Vienna. We also met as a birds-of-a-feather group at FOSDEM for three years in a row (2024, 2025, 2026). When the CNCF restructured its TAGs, the group was closed, and since then I have been looking for ways to keep its spirit alive. Still, none of this gave a newcomer the overview I had wanted back in 2023.

My first attempt at that overview was a talk at Cloud Native Days Italy in May 2026, What Should a Cloud Native OS Look Like?. I realized a talk does not have enough time to cover so many projects in enough detail. It is also much harder not to be biased when speaking in person. In a written document, every claim can link to its source, and separate agents can check each claim against that source. That makes it easier to try to stay objective.

The closest thing I found to a field-wide view is the kube.today survey, Immutable OS for Kubernetes: Where Theory Meets Operations, with answers from 2,138 practitioners. It was sponsored by Spectro Cloud, my employer, and its disclosure says the sponsor had no input on the questions or the analysis. I think it is of great value, because it shows what people actually do. But it does not help much if you are not sure how to compare these projects, or where to start. That is the gap the cloud native OS Pulse tries to close. It is not about sentiment. It records what each project documents and ships, with a source and an evidence label on every cell, plus a few rollback experiments run in VMs.

It is not a scorecard, and it does not pick a winner. My hope is that you find in it a clear view of what image-based, immutable systems buy you and what they cost, which options are out there, and enough perspective to make an informed choice of your own.

The Pulse is a rolling release. The header of every page shows the commit and the date it was built from. When a project ships a newer release than the one profiled, its entry carries a badge that says so until I re-validate it. If you spot something wrong, please open a pull request against that profile, or open an issue. Anyone can.
